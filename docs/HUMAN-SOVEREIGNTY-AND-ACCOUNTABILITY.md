# Human Sovereignty and Accountability Protocol

**Status:** Proposed governance doctrine for Tupua; development-stage policy, not legal advice or a claim of implementation.
**Adopted for design review:** 2026-11-08

## 1. Foundational principle

Technology must increase humanity's capacity to participate in civilization, not eliminate people's ability to influence the institutions that govern them.

The legitimacy of public power depends on law, accountable institutions, meaningful public participation, and the protection of fundamental rights. Automation does not erase those obligations.

## 2. The automation and legitimacy problem

Autonomous systems may reduce an institution's dependence on human labor in administration, logistics, surveillance, revenue collection, and the use of coercive power. This creates risks:
- Decision-makers may become less responsive to the people affected by their decisions.
- Automated enforcement may obscure who is responsible for harmful outcomes.
- Concentrated ownership of compute, data, infrastructure, and automated production may concentrate economic and political influence.
- People may lose meaningful opportunities to contest decisions or participate in shaping public policy.
- Efficiency claims may be used to justify reducing transparency, due process, worker protections, or democratic oversight.

These are risks to assess, not inevitable outcomes. Technology can also improve access to services, public participation, transparency, and worker safety when governed appropriately.

## 3. Non-negotiable safeguards

### A. Human accountability
A named, authorized human institution or official remains accountable for consequential decisions. Responsibility cannot be transferred to an algorithm, model, vendor, or autonomous agent.

### B. Meaningful contestability
People affected by consequential automated decisions must have accessible notice, understandable reasons, a timely path to challenge errors, and review by a competent human decision-maker.

### C. Limits on coercive automation
Systems that support policing, detention, military force, border enforcement, or other coercive state functions require heightened legal scrutiny, strict necessity and proportionality tests, independent oversight, and meaningful human control. Fully autonomous decisions to use force against people are not an acceptable Tupua design objective.

### D. Public transparency
Institutions should disclose when automated systems materially affect public services, eligibility, benefits, employment, liberty, or safety, subject to narrowly tailored protections for legitimate security and privacy interests.

### E. Labor and economic participation
Automation policy should account for displaced workers, job quality, bargaining power, retraining, distribution of productivity gains, and access to the economic value created by shared infrastructure and public investment.

### F. Privacy and data rights
No institution should treat personal data as an unlimited resource. Collection and use must have a lawful basis, a defined purpose, proportional scope, security protections, retention limits, and avenues for redress.

### G. Independent oversight
High-impact systems require independent audits, impact assessments, incident reporting, public-interest review, and the ability to suspend unsafe or unlawful operation.

## 4. Tupua system requirements

Any Tupua feature that influences people, institutions, access to resources, or consequential decisions must:
1. Identify the human owner and accountable decision-maker.
2. Record the purpose, scope, data sources, and limits of the system.
3. Distinguish measured facts, model estimates, and generated content.
4. Explain material recommendations in language appropriate to affected people.
5. Provide human review and appeal paths where consequences are significant.
6. Test for disparate impact, reliability failures, misuse, and security weaknesses.
7. Minimize data collection and restrict access by role and purpose.
8. Preserve audit records while minimizing sensitive personal information.
9. Include a safe pause, rollback, and incident-response mechanism.
10. Never claim that an AI system has legal, political, spiritual, or sovereign authority over people.

## 5. Institutional power and public consent

Tupua should evaluate high-impact technology using these questions:
- Who owns and controls the system?
- Who benefits economically?
- Who bears the risks and costs?
- Whose labor or data makes the system possible?
- Who can inspect, challenge, or stop it?
- What legal authority permits its use?
- What happens when the system fails or causes harm?
- Can affected communities participate in decisions about its deployment?

A system that is efficient but unaccountable is not a successful system.

## 6. Red lines

Tupua must not be used to:
- Suppress lawful public participation, labor organizing, peaceful dissent, or access to due process.
- Enable unlawful surveillance, coercion, discrimination, or retaliation.
- Conceal the use of automation in consequential decisions.
- Evade legal accountability by attributing decisions to an AI.
- Facilitate unauthorized access to, control over, or interference with another party's systems.
- Present speculative capabilities, simulated metrics, or generated claims as verified facts.

## 7. Review and enforcement within Tupua

Before release, each high-impact feature must pass:
- Legal and policy review appropriate to its use.
- Privacy and security assessment.
- Human-rights and disparate-impact assessment.
- Independent technical verification.
- Documented human approval and rollback planning.

Any person responsible for a system may report a concern without retaliation within the project's governance process. Reports must be reviewed and documented; allegations must be assessed on evidence.

## 8. Governing statement

**Human beings are not merely inputs to an automated civilization. They are rights-bearing participants in it. No increase in machine capability diminishes the obligation to preserve human dignity, public accountability, lawful authority, and meaningful consent.**

This protocol governs Tupua's own design and deployment decisions. It is a principled design standard, not a claim that Tupua can govern, command, or override states, companies, or external AI systems.
